Peptide intel

November 2–9, 2026

Published November 9, 2026 · Sources checked against the Federal Register, the HHS policy, the AMA, and FDA

Research use only. This brief reports public regulatory developments. It is not legal advice, medical advice, or compounding guidance. Palm Beach Vitality products are sold for laboratory research only. They are not for human or veterinary use, and they are not compounded medications. The HHS policy below does not approve any peptide, does not place any peptide on the 503A Bulks List, and does not cover research-use-only material.

Must-know

HHS issues an interim enforcement-discretion policy for 503A compounding of six peptides

Federal Register publication October 30, 2026. Notice of availability filed on public inspection October 28. Comments open through November 13, 2026.

HHS, not FDA’s Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, announced a draft Enforcement Discretion Policy Regarding 503A Compounding Using Certain Peptide Bulk Drug Substances (91 FR 68526, Docket HHS-OASH-2026-0496, document 2026-22192). The policy document is on hhs.gov.

While formal rulemaking on the 503A Bulks List continues, HHS says it does not intend to take enforcement action against a state-licensed pharmacy, a federal facility, or a licensed physician compounding a drug from a peptide on Appendix A, if every condition in the policy is met. Appendix A names six peptides: BPC-157, Epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax, and TB-500.

The notice says the policy does not establish rights for any person and is not binding on HHS or the public. HHS may revisit it, in whole or in part, if a safety signal or a product-quality issue arises. It does not add the six peptides to the Bulks List, and it does not make them FDA-approved. State boards of pharmacy can keep stricter rules.

The policy text says the Secretary is trying to displace higher-risk foreign research-use-only supply with a narrower compounding path. That path is limited to 503A compounders who meet the conditions. Research-use-only suppliers are outside it.

The policy lists ten conditions. In short:

  1. The peptide is on Appendix A.
  2. Identity, salt form, and route match the limits in Appendix B.
  3. The bulk substance comes from a facility registered with FDA under section 510 and is accompanied by a valid certificate of analysis covering identity, purity, potency, and specified contaminants.
  4. The original manufacturer is section 510-registered, meets CGMP, and is named on the certificate. A repacker, relabeler, or reseller must document its own vendor and certificate checks.
  5. Serious adverse events and product-quality issues are reported to FDA MedWatch and to the states where the product is compounded and shipped. The patient label explains how to report them.
  6. The label includes: “This is a compounded drug product. Not for resale.”
  7. The compounded product contains only one of the Appendix A active ingredients. Stacks are outside the policy.
  8. An oral dosage form requires documentation that the specific formulation reaches adequate oral bioavailability in humans.
  9. The only device co-packaged with, or containing, the compounded drug is an FDA 510(k)-cleared syringe.
  10. Every other applicable requirement of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act still applies, including a patient-specific prescription and compounding that is not done under insanitary conditions.

The policy cites recent Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee recommendations that favored these peptides, set against CDER scientific staff recommendations against listing them. Comments on docket HHS-OASH-2026-0496 are due November 13, 2026.

Why it matters. This is the largest regulatory development for research-peptide and 503A-adjacent businesses in the November 2–9 window. It opens a temporary, conditional path for patient-specific compounding of compounds that have circulated in research and gray-market channels. It is not clearance. Research-use-only material, and compounding that misses the conditions, stays fully exposed. A safety or quality problem can end the policy.

AMA criticizes the HHS policy

November 6, 2026 · American Medical Association, National Advocacy Update

In the November 6 National Advocacy Update, AMA President Willie Underwood III, MD, MSc, MPH, said a policy that “effectively authorizes the compounding of non-FDA-approved substances puts patients at increased risk for serious, preventable harm.” He also said: “Allowing pharmacies to compound unapproved synthetic peptide substances without sufficient evidence of their safety and efficacy puts patients at risk.”

The update names the same six substances: BPC-157, Epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax, and TB-500. It points to an earlier AMA letter to FDA describing incomplete characterization, pharmacokinetics, toxicology, dosing, routes, and quality standards for these synthetic peptides.

Why it matters. Organized medical-society opposition can influence state boards of pharmacy, liability questions for compounding clinics, and the later formal rulemaking. The policy is contested. It is not regulatory clearance.

Also on the radar

FDA requires an NAION warning on approved GLP-1 labels

November 9, 2026 · FDA Drug Safety Communication

FDA is requiring Warnings and Precautions language for all approved GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide and tirzepatide products, because clinical-trial, Sentinel, and post-marketing data show a potential increased risk of rare non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION). NAION can cause sudden, painless vision loss in one eye. The communication is here: FDA Drug Safety Communication, November 9, 2026.

Why it matters. This applies to FDA-approved GLP-1 drugs. It does not change the 503A or 503B status of the six peptides above, and it does not change the status of retatrutide. It is a spillover signal for the broader peptide and GLP-1 compounding and gray-market space, where unapproved versions still circulate, and it is another sign of post-market safety scrutiny on the class.

What did not move this week

Inside the strict November 2–9 window, this review did not find a new peer-reviewed human study, a substantive new U.S. ClinicalTrials.gov registration, new Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee activity, a shortage-list change, or a fresh FDA warning letter on the peptides covered above.

Left out on purpose: recycled coverage of the earlier PCAC votes and older warning letters, ClinicalTrials.gov records that describe themselves as fictional or mock, older BPC-157 and TB-500 reviews published before this window, influencer and affiliate stack content, and coverage of the HHS policy dated before November 2.
